# Agent 2: What Goes Wrong — Clause Types That Burn People (Evidence Review)

Research budget used: 12/12 web searches, 6 page fetches (2 of which returned HTTP 403
from ftc.gov and yielded no content — URLs from those still cited where a snippet was
confirmed via search results). Stopped once 8+ distinct sourced findings were secured,
per instructions.

Scope note: FTC.gov press-release pages block direct fetching (403), so several FTC
findings below are sourced from FTC.gov URLs surfaced and quoted in search results,
or corroborated via secondary reporting (CBS News, Holland & Knight, Legal Dive) rather
than a direct page fetch. URLs are still real and traceable.

---

## 1. Ranked List of Clause Types (by evidence volume + severity found)

1. **Auto-renewal / cancellation-friction clauses** — By far the deepest evidence
   trail: three separate multi-million/billion-dollar FTC enforcement actions in the
   last few years (Amazon Prime, Match.com, Chegg) plus an active FTC lawsuit against
   LA Fitness, plus survey data suggesting ~42% of Americans have complained about
   cancellation difficulty. This is the single most litigated/enforced clause pattern
   found.

2. **Non-compete / restrictive covenant clauses** — Second-deepest evidence: a
   federal rulemaking effort that drew 26,000+ public comments (96% in support of a
   ban), plus a concrete enforcement case (Prudential Security) with a specific harmed
   population (1,000+ minimum-wage security guards) and a quantifiable threat ($100,000
   liquidated-damages penalty for breach). Severity is high because it affects ability
   to earn a living, not just money.

3. **Early termination penalties / liquidated damages** — Shows up as a recurring
   severity-multiplier layered inside other categories rather than standing fully
   alone in the evidence: gym contracts (LA Fitness), the Prudential non-compete's
   $100k liquidated-damages clause, and lease termination fees "that can total
   thousands" (Greystar). Ranked here because the dollar-harm evidence is concrete and
   repeats across contract types.

4. **Fee/rent escalators & undisclosed mandatory fees (leases)** — A live state AG
   lawsuit (Colorado v. Greystar) with concrete allegations of hidden recurring fees
   and lease terms consumers can't see until after paying an application fee. Strong,
   but currently a single major case rather than a pattern of many.

5. **IP assignment / work-for-hire grabs (freelance contracts)** — Well documented in
   trade journalism (Columbia Journalism Review) with named-source legal commentary
   describing publishers claiming perpetual, global, all-platform rights at
   non-professional rates. Real and recurring, but the evidence is more "widely
   known professional problem" than "regulatory action with dollar figures."

6. **Indemnification clauses** — Same CJR source plus small-business legal commentary
   describe freelancers/small vendors taking on uncapped liability for a client's
   legal defense costs. Severity can be catastrophic (unlimited exposure) but the
   evidence found is expert commentary/legal-guidance rather than a specific
   dollar-harm incident.

7. **Data/privacy rights grabs** — Concrete FTC cases exist (Flo Health, Premom)
   involving millions of users' reproductive health data shared without consent in
   violation of stated privacy terms. Real harm, real settlements, but this is more
   about privacy-policy misrepresentation than a single "gotcha clause" in the
   contract text itself.

8. **Arbitration / class-action waiver clauses** — One very concrete, widely reported
   real incident (Disney invoking a Disney+ trial's arbitration clause to try to block
   a wrongful-death lawsuit) plus a large body of general consumer-law commentary
   about buried arbitration clauses. Ranked lower because most of the supporting
   material is explanatory/legal-guidance rather than incident-specific.

9. **Liability caps (limitation of liability)** — Evidence found is almost entirely
   legal-guidance/how-to-negotiate content aimed at drafters, not documented consumer
   or freelancer harm tied to a specific real incident. Flagging as thin evidence
   (see "What I could not find").

10. **Unilateral termination rights (platform account bans, no recourse)** — Weakest
    evidence found: mostly definitional/marketing-tool content (contract-clause
    comparison sites) and unverified forum anecdotes (Roblox dev forum posts). One
    real data point (Apple App Store's own P2B complaint report: 48 complaints, 43/48
    upheld in the platform's favor) actually undercuts the "epidemic" framing rather
    than supporting it. Flagging as thin evidence.

## 2. Sourced Findings

### Auto-renewal / cancellation friction

- **Amazon Prime** — FTC alleged Amazon used dark patterns to enroll consumers in
  Prime and made cancellation deliberately difficult; case resulted in **$2.5 billion**
  in monetary relief and civil penalties, plus a requirement for clear auto-renewal
  disclosures and simple, unobstructed cancellation.
  Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule-struck-down

- **Match.com** — FTC secured a **$14 million** settlement over deceptive free-trial
  promises, suspending accounts of consumers who disputed charges, and confusing
  cancellation procedures.
  Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule-struck-down

- **Chegg Inc.** — FTC settlement of **$7.5 million** for continuing to charge
  consumers after they attempted to cancel, and for a cancellation flow that was
  "confusing and cumbersome" and hard to locate online.
  Source: https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/09/ftc-steps-up-subscription-enforcement-after-click-to-cancel-rule-struck-down

- **LA Fitness** — FTC sued LA Fitness's operators alleging the gym made cancellation
  "exceedingly difficult," restricting it to specific times or requiring a
  conversation with specific managers who were often unavailable; "tens of thousands"
  of customers reportedly complained.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/08/ftc-sues-la-fitness-making-it-difficult-consumers-cancel-gym-memberships

- **General complaint volume** — Cited survey finding that roughly 42% of Americans
  have complained about companies making the cancellation process needlessly
  difficult, and 59% report being funneled from a free/low-fee trial into an
  expensive ongoing contract without clear informed consent.
  Source: https://nclnet.org/ftc_autorenew/

### Non-compete / restrictive covenant clauses

- **Prudential Security (Michigan)** — FTC action against a security-guard staffing
  company that required **1,000+ minimum-wage security guards** to sign non-competes
  barring them from working for a competitor within 100 miles for two years, backed
  by a **$100,000 liquidated-damages** penalty clause; the company sued departing
  guards who took higher-paying jobs elsewhere. A Michigan state court had already
  found the restriction unreasonable and unenforceable before the FTC acted.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-approves-final-order-requiring-michigan-based-security-companies-drop-noncompete-restrictions
  Corroborating: https://cbsnews.com/amp/news/noncompete-agreement-feds-sue-3-companies-that-prevented-employees-from-leaving-for-competitors and https://www.legaldive.com/news/ftc-noncompete-complaint-noncompete-ban-prudential-security-command-unfair-practices-non-competes/645510/

- **Scale of public reaction** — FTC's 2023 proposed rule to ban non-competes drew
  more than **26,000 public comments**, with over 25,000 (roughly 96%) in support of
  banning the practice — evidence of very high documented public grievance volume.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-proposes-rule-ban-noncompete-clauses-which-hurt-workers-harm-competition

### Fee/rent escalators & undisclosed lease fees

- **Greystar (Colorado AG lawsuit)** — Colorado's Attorney General sued alleging
  Greystar advertised "deceptively low rental prices" while failing to disclose
  mandatory recurring fees (valet trash, package delivery, amenities, pest control)
  that renters must pay even if unused, costing tenants "hundreds, if not thousands,
  of dollars more than they anticipated." Total costs often aren't visible until
  after an applicant has already paid a non-refundable application fee. The suit also
  alleges lease-termination fees that "can total thousands."
  Source: https://www.yahoo.com/news/colorado-attorney-general-accuses-greystar-202604014.html

### IP assignment / work-for-hire (freelance contracts)

- **Freelance journalism work-for-hire clauses** — Columbia Journalism Review reports
  publishers increasingly demanding "global rights across all platforms" at
  non-professional rates; under work-for-hire terms the publisher owns the content
  outright and can resell/adapt it with no further payment to the creator, even for
  future platforms/technology not yet invented. Media lawyer Jonathan Peters is
  quoted describing the risk and recommending freelancers cap exposure rather than
  accept blanket work-for-hire terms.
  Source: https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/freelance-contracts.php

### Indemnification clauses

- **Freelance/small-business indemnification exposure** — Same CJR reporting
  describes indemnity clauses that make a freelancer responsible for a publisher's
  legal defense costs arising from the freelancer's own work (e.g., defamation,
  copyright, privacy claims), with Peters recommending a liability cap proportional
  to pay ("If you're getting $1,000 for the story, you shouldn't have $1 million of
  liability").
  Source: https://www.cjr.org/watchdog/freelance-contracts.php

### Data / privacy rights grabs

- **Flo Health (period-tracking app)** — A 2019 Wall Street Journal investigation
  found Flo was sharing users' personal health data with third parties (including
  Facebook's and Google's analytics divisions, AppsFlyer, Flurry) despite its privacy
  policy promising the data would not be shared — leading to an FTC settlement
  requiring independent privacy-practice review and affirmative user consent before
  future sharing.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/01/developer-popular-womens-fertility-tracking-app-settles-ftc-allegations-it-misled-consumers-about

- **Premom (ovulation-tracking app)** — FTC charged the developer with sharing
  users' sensitive health data with third parties, including two China-based firms,
  without notifying consumers, in violation of the Health Breach Notification Rule;
  proposed order bars the company from sharing health data for advertising purposes.
  Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/05/ovulation-tracking-app-premom-will-be-barred-sharing-health-data-advertising-under-proposed-ftc

### Arbitration / class-action waiver clauses

- **Disney+ arbitration clause invoked in a wrongful-death case** — Reporting
  describes Disney attempting to compel arbitration in a wrongful-death lawsuit by
  pointing to an arbitration clause the plaintiff's husband had agreed to years
  earlier while signing up for a free Disney+ trial — used as the primary real-world
  example of how a terms-of-service arbitration clause can strip away litigation
  rights in a context totally unrelated to what the person thought they were agreeing
  to.
  Source: https://cbs12.com/news/local/terms-of-service-agreements-are-you-unknowingly-signing-away-your-rights

## 3. What I Could Not Find (gaps, given the search/read budget)

- **Liability-cap clauses**: I found abundant legal-guidance content explaining how
  limitation-of-liability clauses work in SaaS/vendor agreements, but no specific,
  named real-world incident (a consumer or small business harmed and unable to
  recover damages because of a liability cap) with a citable source. This category
  is likely real-world painful but I could not substantiate it with incident-level
  evidence in the searches run — treat as a hypothesis needing more targeted search
  (e.g., specific court cases enforcing SaaS liability caps against harmed customers).

- **Unilateral termination rights (e.g., "we can terminate/suspend your account at
  our sole discretion")**: evidence was thin and low-quality — mostly
  contract-clause-comparison marketing sites and unverified developer-forum
  complaints (Roblox), not journalism, regulator action, or legal-aid casework. The
  one hard data point found (Apple's own published complaint-handling numbers: 48
  App Store termination complaints, company upheld its own decision in 43 of 48) does
  not clearly support "this is a major recurring pain point" — it may simply reflect
  low complaint volume or an appeals process stacked in the platform's favor. Flagging
  rather than asserting a ranking claim here.

- **General ToS/data-privacy "rights grab" language** (broad content licenses,
  behavioral tracking) beyond the two FTC health-app cases: found generic explanatory
  content (e.g., Cambridge Analytica name-checked in an unfetched search snippet) but
  could not confirm a specific citable URL tying that exact claim to a source within
  budget, so it was dropped per the "no URL, drop it" rule.

- Ran out of search/read budget before digging into **escalator clauses in
  commercial vs. residential leases separately**, or **freelance non-payment /
  kill-fee** patterns as their own category (adjacent to IP assignment/indemnification
  findings above but not separately evidenced).
